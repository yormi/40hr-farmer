# Month 2: Take Control of Plant Vigor (Brainstorm Source)

> Purpose: raw, deduped lever-by-lever source material for the Month 2 video on steering vegetative/generative balance on purpose.
> Source: Andrew Mefferd, *The Greenhouse and Hoophouse Grower's Handbook*.
> Status: DO NOT PUBLISH. Brainstorm source only. Stage 1 (single-lens) extraction.

Plant vigor is the balance between vegetative (leaf/stem) and generative (fruit/flower) growth, read off visible signals and steered on purpose. Every bullet below is the book lens.

## Reading vigor

- Read the head, the top 4 to 8 inches (10 to 20 cm) of new growth, to judge state: a thin stem with a flower cluster close to the head reads generative; a thick stem with lots of growth above the cluster reads vegetative. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Run weekly crop registration: tag and number set plants, then measure weekly growth, stem diameter 6 inches (15 cm) below the tip with calipers, distance from tip to newest cluster, and flowers set. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Read the registration trend, not a snapshot: slowing weekly growth plus thinning stems signals a generative shift; accelerating growth plus thickening stems signals vegetative. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Judge stem caliper against the trend, but as rough reference at the flowering cluster a 0.75 inch (1.87 cm) stem reads vegetative, 0.5 inch (1.25 cm) reads balanced. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Read a vegetative tomato by long, thick, dark leaves, an oversized oval stem below the head, and stiff trusses standing straight up instead of arching out. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Read a generative tomato by a thin head stem, darker yellow flowers, short well proportioned arching trusses, smaller flatter less curled leaves, and flowers opening near the tip. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Read the highest open flower: far below the tip means vegetative; at or close to the tip (within about 3 inches / 7.5 cm) means generative. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Touch a leaf: an active plant feels cooler than the air; a hot leaf means stomata are closing and the plant is near wilting, so add water or fix irrigation. [Andrew] (Ch5, Ch7)
- Watch for vegetative drift right after transplant and after poor fruit set, when no fruit load exists to absorb the plant's energy; fruit load on a mature plant balances it. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Take regular refractometer brix readings on plant sap; a steady drop signals something going wrong with the plant. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Read adventitious roots forming on the stem away from soil contact as a stress signal. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Read early-season fruit deformities (catfacing, zippering, fused king fruit) as cold stress during hidden flower development; count sepals, more than five means multiple flowers fused. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Read a too-generative cucumber by longer, larger fruit. [Andrew] (Ch10)
- Read burnt cucumber heads (dry, curled, burned new leaves and tendrils) as interrupted calcium flow from stress: too hot/cold, too wet/dry, low humidity, excess fertility, or heavy fruit load; rule out mites. [Andrew] (Ch10)
- Read pepper fruit maturity by feel: ripe peppers gain size before wall thickness, so a gentle squeeze separates thick-walled mature fruit from thin-walled undermature. [Andrew] (Ch11)
- Read hot pepper heat as a stress signal; stressed plants produce hotter peppers. [Andrew] (Ch11)
- Read bolting in greens as the vegetative-to-generative switch: stem elongates into a pine-tree shape and the crop turns bitter. To confirm, halve a head lengthwise: a short disk-shaped stalk means vegetative, an elongated core means it has gone generative. [Andrew] (Ch13)
- Suspect potassium starvation of fruit after prolonged cool overcast spells or hot weather following cool, since slow transpiration and reduced roots cannot supply the fruit. [Andrew] (Ch9)

## Climate steering (general)

- Read available light to decide what a structure can carry: leafy crops want 12 to 14 mol/m2/day, vining/fruiting crops need 20 to 30; small seedlings need only 6 to 9. Low light grows leaves but not fruit, so a dark-season transplant can ride rising light into production. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Keep plants in continual active growth; once a crop stalls from cold or any stress it takes a long while to recover, so do not move plants into a too-cold hoophouse. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Change greenhouse temperature gradually, no faster than 2F (1C) per hour, to avoid shocking plants. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- When a crop is far to one side, stack multiple steering techniques at once and expect at least a few days, not overnight, for effects to show. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Twist the stem around the string for a slightly more generative push; clip plants you want more vegetative. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Top the plant (cut the growing point) near season end so upward growth stops and all energy goes to ripening the existing fruit load. Topped about two months before removal. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Drop pre-night temperature as low as 61F (16C) to steer tomatoes generative. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Read early-season blossom end rot as a vegetative-vigor signal; use generative steering to pull the plant less vegetative. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Match growing temperature to available light; running a crop hot under low light burns more energy in respiration than weak photosynthesis can replace. [Andrew] (Ch5, Ch13)

## Temperature and DIF

- Set the 24-hour average temperature to the crop's recommended value; plants respond to the average, not extremes, so day and night should average to that number. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Read morphology off the 24-hour average: cooler averages give tight internodes, larger flowers, thicker stems and leaves; warmer averages give long internodes and smaller flowers, stems, leaves. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Steer with the day/night differential: a flat profile is most vegetative; the wider the day-to-night gap, the more generative the push. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Build a day/night differential for fruiting crops; a flat profile, even at the fast-growth 77F (25C), runs them vegetative at the expense of fruit. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Encourage vegetative growth and fast rooting in the first week after transplant by running a high species temperature with little or no day/night swing. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Even out a too-hot day by dropping night temperature, but never below the crop minimum; a 100F day cannot be balanced by a 40F night without shutting plants down. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Hold heated houses at or above the crop minimum night temperature; skimping on night heat cold-stresses photosynthesis for multiple days and costs more production than the fuel saved. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Do not let night temperatures run hotter than necessary; excess night warmth burns energy that would otherwise fund growth. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Keep plants inside their active range at all times; below the night low they shut down and respiration stalls, delaying the morning restart. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Treat 96F (36C) as the ceiling for vining crops; above it respiration outpaces photosynthesis and time there produces no growth. [Andrew] (Ch5, Ch7, Ch9)
- Avoid running plants hot on low-light cloudy days; high temperature drives respiration that burns energy faster than weak photosynthesis replaces it. [Andrew] (Ch5)

### Pre-day and pre-night treatments

- Run a pre-day: start warming hours before sunrise so the house hits target at dawn instead of letting the sun spike it (e.g. raise 6F over 3 hours to hit 68F at 7am). For fruiting crops start even earlier, a half hour to an hour before sunrise, so the fruits warm and can take assimilates as soon as photosynthesis starts. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Run a pre-night to drive energy to fruit: at day's end keep vents open and let temperature drop as fast as possible to the night low, breaking the 2F/hour rule. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Intensify a pre-night by widening the spread: ramp a few degrees above daytime at day's end, then drop a few degrees below the night average and prolong time at the low. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Use pre-nights routinely on vegetative-prone tomatoes and eggplant, and on peppers to fix blossom drop; skip them on cucumbers unless needed. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Only run pre-nights on sunny days with surplus assimilates; on cloudy days or overcast runs they thin the head and lose vigor, and never use them on an already generative crop. [Andrew] (Ch7)

### Germination temperature

- Hold germination temperature constant from planting to emergence; the nighttime dip in most greenhouses throws germination off even when daytime temps are fine, so use a germinator or bottom heat. [Andrew] (Ch6)

## Irrigation

- Always meet the plant's water needs; drought stress closes stomata, halting transpiration, photosynthesis, and growth. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Water only while the plant is active: start two hours after sunrise, stop two hours before sunset, and avoid overnight watering. In very arid air, allow minimal night watering only as needed to prevent water stress. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Steer with watering: many small evenly spaced irrigations keep roots uniformly moist and push vegetative; fewer larger irrigations with more drydown push generative. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Push generative by starting irrigation later in the day and ending the last irrigation earlier, leaving a longer overnight drydown. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Manipulate root pressure with water and EC: high moisture plus low root-zone EC pushes root pressure up, high EC holds it down. Keep root pressure from running high to avoid guttation (wet leaves invite disease), so do not overwater and do not water at night. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Start irrigating fruiting crops shortly after transplant to drive rapid root establishment. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Apply less water and fertility to grafted plants and expect similar results; the larger root system draws from deeper. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- On very hot days expect some cucumber wilting and respond by getting plants more water before prolonged wilt kills leaf tissue. [Andrew] (Ch10)
- Keep cucumber root-zone moisture and temperature even to prevent crooked fruit; wide swings cause crooking. [Andrew] (Ch10)
- Do not overwater tomatoes to chase yield past about 60 kg/m2/yr; water pumped into fruit above that dilutes sugars and flavor. [Andrew] (Ch9)

## Feeding and EC

- Do not apply a whole season's fertilizer up front; the nitrogen load pushes vining crops excessively vegetative. Incorporate half or a third and feed the rest gradually. [Andrew] (Ch5, Ch7)
- Avoid excessive nitrogen, which makes plants too vegetative; soil-grown crops respond slowly, so fertility is not the fastest lever. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Raise root-zone EC for a generative effect; trial in steps of plus or minus 0.5 mS/cm from your normal EC, noting higher EC long term may raise flavor and cut yield. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Run tissue tests every week or two to catch a nutrient running low before deficiency symptoms appear and cost production. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Feed extra potassium through fertigation or side dressing during heavy fruit ripening to prevent gray wall, blotchy ripening, and yellow shoulder; ripening fruit needs more than one up-front application. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Remove blossom-end-rot fruit as soon as seen since they cannot recover; elongated Romas and San Marzanos are more susceptible. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Avoid overfertilizing into lush soft growth, which is more botrytis-prone than balanced growth; also keep above 65F (18C) with good airflow and no pruning stubs. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Avoid excess nitrogen on greens at any stage; too much makes growth lush and more susceptible to pests and pathogens. [Andrew] (Ch13)

## CO2

- Keep CO2 from becoming the limiting factor; in closed cold-season houses it depletes and stalls growth, so vent for fresh air or supplement. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Supplement CO2 to 800 to 1,000 ppm to lift photosynthesis up to 50 percent over ambient; start shortly before sunrise and stop shortly before sunset, since CO2 is consumed only in light. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Raise CO2 for a more generative effect. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- In dark-season propagation raise CO2 to 700 to 800 ppm and supplement light to speed seedling growth and shorten time to transplant. [Andrew] (Ch11)

## Humidity

- Steer humidity to a happy medium where transpiration is active but not too fast: VPD between 4 and 8 mbar, or RH between 70 and 80 percent. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Steer with humidity for balance: raise it within the crop's acceptable range to push vegetative, lower it to push generative since the plant works harder to transpire. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Avoid chronically high RH and low VPD; it weakens root systems because the plant is not stimulated to grow roots to feed transpiration. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Read VPD-driven morphology: humid air gives larger, thicker leaves and stems; dry air gives smaller, thinner ones. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Expect temporary "lazy root" wilting after an overcast spell followed by hot sun; plants usually outgrow it as roots catch up to water demand. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Power vent on cool humid mornings by setting the vent point a degree or two below the heater just long enough to expel excess humidity and keep the crop active. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Prevent condensation proactively by keeping temperature above the dew point; once plants are already wet it is too late. [Andrew] (Ch7)

## Variety and rootstock

- Pick variety to counter your environment: in a naturally vegetative environment choose a more generative or easily balanced variety. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Choose a true greenhouse variety for higher yield over a longer season; even one extra fruit over a field variety can cover the seed premium. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Trial varieties under your own conditions and take yield data; a high-yielding diva may underperform, and some scrappy types go vegetative under optimal conditions. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Avoid seedling-stage stress, especially low temperatures, since flowers form in the growing point early and stress there hurts the first fruit. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Use a real rootstock variety, not a cheap disease-resistant fruiting variety; even the most vigorous fruiting variety is less vigorous than a true rootstock, so you lose the vigor and yield boost. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Choose interspecific-cross rootstocks for hybrid vigor; the more distantly related the parents, the stronger the effect. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Match rootstock vigor to your top and conditions; pairing a very vigorous top with a very vigorous rootstock can over-vigor the combination, so trial new combinations first. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Expect roughly a 30 to 50 percent yield increase from grafting under adverse conditions, closer to 10 percent near optimal; the worse the conditions, the bigger the rootstock payoff. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Graft a weak or generative top variety onto a strong rootstock to boost its vigor and carry it through difficult conditions to season's end. [Andrew] (Ch6, Ch8)
- Pick tomato rootstocks on disease resistance plus vigor; treat a broad disease package as cheap insurance even without known active root disease. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- In hot summer climates pick a vigorous tomato variety or graft to a vigorous rootstock, since prolonged heat weakens plants. [Andrew] (Ch9)

## Grafting

- Graft to use two seeds (rootstock plus top variety) to build one double-leader plant capable of heavy fruit off each leader (up to 30 lb / 14 kg per leader in a good year). [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Expect most rootstocks to push grafted plants more vegetative and harder to balance early; try lower-vigor balanced rootstocks for naturally vegetative types like small-fruited tomatoes and eggplant. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Expect single-leader grafted plants to be harder to balance; most carry at least two heads to absorb the extra vigor. [Andrew] (Ch7, Ch9)
- If grafted plants go excessively vegetative at the expense of fruit, apply crop steering to rebalance. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Cull grafted plants that grow slower than their flatmates; they healed only enough to live, not to grow well, and never reach full potential (check after a week or two). [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Match scion and rootstock stem diameters as closely as possible when top grafting; closer match means higher success. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Target stems of 1.5 to 3 mm (1.5 to 2 mm when learning), avoiding below 1.2 mm and oversized woody stems; smaller stems carry less leaf area, transpire less, and wilt slower after cutting. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Slip a target-size grafting clip onto an intact stem; when it fits snug, the seedling has reached grafting size. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Do a small test planting of each variety a few weeks before grafting and note how fast each reaches grafting size, then stagger sowing so stems meet at the same size. Sow cherry and grape scions a few days earlier (thinner stems). [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Add two weeks to the propagation timeline for grafted transplants since growth halts at grafting; plant a ten-week seedling where you would plant an eight-week one. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Overseed when grafting (50 to 100 percent typical) to cover poor germination, stem mismatch, and graft losses; sow heirlooms two to four times the final count. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Water grafting candidates well the day before so the medium is moist but not sopping; overly wet rootstocks pass too much moisture to heal properly. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Move seedlings to a cooler, less sunny spot a few hours before grafting to slow respiration so they wilt slower after cutting. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Graft out of direct sunlight at a 70 to 75F (21 to 24C) work area; light and heat drive faster respiration and wilting. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Remove some leaves before grafting on leafy seedlings to cut respiration; as a last resort strip all leaves off a wilting batch but always leave the growing point. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Cut the graft union at least 1 inch (2.5 cm) above the medium (1 to 1.5 inches if leggy) to keep the scion from rooting into the soil. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Cut both stems at a steep 60 to 70 degree angle for more healing surface, and match the two cuts as precisely as possible. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Look for a drop of moisture beading from the cut stem; that upward flow from rootstock to scion is a good sign the graft will heal. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Graft only as many at once as you can move into the healing chamber before the first ones wilt; never let newly grafted plants wilt. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Hold 95 percent plus humidity, complete darkness, and 80 to 82F (26 to 28C) for the first 24 to 48 hours, where scar tissue forms fastest. At 100 percent humidity transpiration stops and moisture stays in the leaves until roots resupply it. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- After 24 to 48 hours keep humidity high but reintroduce dim (half-strength) light and drop temperature to 72 to 77F (22 to 25C), holding this low-light medium-temp high-humidity stage three more days. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Ensure healing light comes from more than one direction; one-sided light makes plants lean and pull their graft unions apart. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Wean off the chamber by making the smallest opening and reading wilt: no wilt means advance next day, wilt means rehumidify and back up a step, reducing humidity gradually over three to four days. Never skip more than one acclimation step. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Do not over-heal; plants in the chamber too long develop adventitious scion roots, rot, and become peaked. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Reintroduce healed plants on a mild day, early morning before heat or with reduced airflow, and check them an hour or two later. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Read adventitious scion roots as a stress signal or poor healing; do not let them develop or they expose the plant to soilborne disease the rootstock would have resisted. [Andrew] (Ch8, Ch9)
- For cucurbit one-cotyledon grafts, leave one cotyledon on the rootstock as a carbohydrate source so the plant survives healing. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Confirm a cucurbit rootstock does not impart off flavors or alter bloom on the scion fruit; trial before relying on it. [Andrew] (Ch8, Ch10)
- Cucumber and pepper rootstocks give soilborne disease protection but no tomato-style yield boost, so test on a small scale; pepper grafting is not yet worth it commercially. [Andrew] (Ch10, Ch11)

## Pruning

- Limit each plant to a controlled head count; an unlimited count splits finite energy so growth and ripening slow and fruit sizes down. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Prune to keep foliage and fruit balanced; balanced plants ripen, size fruit faster, and yield more predictably. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Steer with leaf pruning: more pruning is stressful and generative, more leaves is vegetative, and aggressive pruning from the start can settle a crop that began too vegetative. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- On indeterminate tomatoes, remove the leaf opposite a flower cluster when it is about 1 inch (2.5 cm) long to redirect energy to the cluster and neighbors. Taking a leaf out of the head is generative; do it on vegetative tomatoes from as early as the first cluster, stop first as the plant rebalances, and never on peppers or cucumbers. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Remove mature leaves below the fruit on tomatoes and eggplant to raise airflow, cut disease, and ripen fruit faster; old shaded leaves contribute little photosynthesis. [Andrew] (Ch6, Ch12)
- Cut any removed part back to a living stem, never leave a stub; a stub rots back into the stem and infects the plant. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Prune in the morning when plants are turgid so they snap cleanly and wounds dry through the day; trellis in the afternoon when plants flex without breaking. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Sucker on time; a sucker left too long grows as big as the main stem and competes for energy. Snap suckers off by hand at about 1 inch (2.5 cm); cut only large ones that will not snap. [Andrew] (Ch6, Ch9)
- Never strip all suckers from the top until topping; always keep a few small suckers near the head as backup replacements. [Andrew] (Ch9, Ch10)
- Never trellis above the mature leaves; clipping or twisting still-expanding growth hinders upward growth, so look down the plant to gauge mature leaf size first. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Trellis on a regular schedule before plants flop: weekly for tomatoes and eggplant, more often for cucumbers, every other week for slower peppers. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Remove any sucker below the graft union; it is rootstock and produces no usable fruit while competing with the main stem. [Andrew] (Ch8)
- Transplant grafted plants with the graft union an inch (2.5 cm) or more above the soil line, never below it, so the scion cannot root in. [Andrew] (Ch8, Ch9)

## Fruit load

- Prune flowering or fruiting clusters to match fruit set to what the plant can mature; all fruiting crops set more than they can carry. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Leave more fruit on for a generative effect; on a high fruit load growing slowly, harvest harder to send energy back to the head. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- When the plant has too many sinks (over-pruned or disease-killed leaves), reduce fruit load by harvesting harder or removing an immature fruit or cluster. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Lighten the fruit load any time plants are struggling, accepting a short-term yield dip to rebalance them. [Andrew] (Ch7)

## Density and leaders

- Space plants and wires as tightly as you would ever want them; you can always cut density later by widening in-row spacing but cannot easily tighten fixed wires. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Step density up through the season as light rises by developing a second then third leader, and top extras in fall as light drops. Develop the sucker just below the first flower cluster into the second vine, since it is the strongest and best matches the primary. [Andrew] (Ch5, Ch6, Ch9)
- Increase plant spacing through dark months to reduce mutual shading and keep plants growing on marginal light. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Add leaders to bleed off an overly vigorous plant; use a single stem to consolidate and maintain a weak variety. Grow most ungrafted tomatoes single-leader, vigorous grafted plants on two. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- On a low greenhouse run more leaders (up to four on eggplant) at the same spacing; more leaders reach the wire more slowly, suiting short structures. [Andrew] (Ch6, Ch12)
- On multi-headed small-fruited plants choose suckers near the top, since shaded low suckers grow slowly and never catch up; watch that too many stems slow the plant. [Andrew] (Ch9, Ch12)
- Lower and lean long-season vines to grow a plant of almost unlimited length, letting out about a foot of twine at a time; move the head sideways in the lean direction so the stem keeps tension and does not kink and snap. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Set head density by fruit size, counting heads not plants: grape 2.8 to 5.6, cherry 4.2, beefsteak 3.15 heads/m2; space smaller-fruited varieties tighter and larger ones wider (bigger leaves cast more shade). [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Bury ungrafted tomato seedlings deeper than their cell soil line to form adventitious roots and a larger root system at planting. [Andrew] (Ch9)

## Crop-specific notes

### Tomatoes (Ch9)

- Hold a flat 73 to 77F (23 to 25C) day and night the first week after transplant for rapid rooting; then 75 to 76F day / 64 to 66F night, then 75 to 80F day / 64 to 67F night once fully loaded with fruit. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Keep the house under 86F (30C) and above 60F (15C); below 60F interferes with hidden flower development (catfacing, zippering, fused fruit) and below 48F causes serious setbacks. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Keep the structure cool through at least the morning before adverse heat; above the mid-90sF before noon risks sterilizing flowers that opened that morning. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Keep root-zone temperature 64 to 70F (18 to 21C); below 61F (16C) brings serious growth reductions. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Germinate at a steady 80 to 82F (27 to 28C), then a flat 66 to 68F (19 to 20C) day and night for two to three weeks until transplant. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- On truss cherries, nip the last few fruits off the tip when more set than the plant can fill so each remaining fruit fills out fully. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Prune determinates no higher than the sucker below the first flower cluster; more pruning cuts their limited fruit-setting chances. [Andrew] (Ch9)

### Cucumbers (Ch10)

- Treat cucumbers as fast growers (mature plants grow 4 inches / 10 cm or more per day in heat); prune or trellis more than once a week, and pick cocktail types once a day or more so they do not overgrow. [Andrew] (Ch10)
- Hold a 24-hour average of 70F (21C); keep above 61 to 63F (16 to 17C) or plants shut down, and avoid above 86F (30C) where transpiration outruns watering. Keep root zone 65 to 68F (18 to 20C). [Andrew] (Ch10)
- Run a flat 73 to 77F day and night the first week, then drop nights to 70 to 73F; at harvest start run days 70 to 75F / nights 63 to 68F, fully loaded days 75 to 80F / nights 63 to 70F. [Andrew] (Ch10)
- Grow gynoecious parthenocarpic varieties under cover so every flower sets without pollination; prevent pollination to keep fruit seedless and crunchy. [Andrew] (Ch10)
- For larger-fruited (7 in / 18 cm and up) choose single-fruited types and allow one fruit per node; for small-fruited choose multifruited types and let them set freely unless plants look weak. [Andrew] (Ch10)
- For umbrella, prefer selective varieties that abort under heavy load; for high-wire, use non-selective varieties so you decide which fruits to keep. Prune to limit fruit fed at once, smoothing flushy set-and-abort cycles and keeping the plant vigorous longer. [Andrew] (Ch10)
- On large-fruited cultivars remove the first flowers and set no fruit on the bottom 2 ft (60 cm) of vine so the plant bulks up first; remove more early flowers in winter or on weak plants. [Andrew] (Ch10)
- For high-wire keep one stem with one flower per node; when stressed or too generative, remove every other flower to push energy into vegetative growth. [Andrew] (Ch10)
- Snap suckers and tendrils off cleanly at 1 to 2 in (2.5 to 5 cm); break at least half of each tendril so it cannot grab and scar fruit. [Andrew] (Ch10)
- On umbrella plants let a cuke set every other node up to the wire in sunny months, as few as three below the wire in dark winter; leave three suckers below the wire, top just above it to push energy into those laterals, then pinch laterals at chest height. [Andrew] (Ch10)
- Run umbrella at 1.2 to 1.8 plants/m2, high-wire at about half tomato density (1.8 plants/m2, up to 3.6 in high light); transplant at 3 to 5 true leaves before root-bound. [Andrew] (Ch10)
- Prevent morning fruit burn by eliminating overnight condensation with heat and warming the house gradually to hit day temperature by sunrise. [Andrew] (Ch10)

### Peppers (Ch11)

- Match variety to greenhouse type: generative high-tech (Dutch) varieties for heated houses, vegetative low-tech (Mexican/Spanish) varieties for simpler houses that run into stress. In an unheated cold-area tunnel, skip greenhouse pruning and grow a field variety on a low trellis with little or no pruning. [Andrew] (Ch11)
- Hold a 24-hour average of 70 to 72F (21 to 22C); never drop below 61F (16C) or the plant shuts down, and try not to exceed 82F (28C). [Andrew] (Ch11)
- Germinate at a constant 82F (28C), then seedlings day 70 to 75F / night 68 to 69F. At the king flower start a generative regime (day 72 to 75F / night 66 to 67F). After transplant run a warm narrow DIF (day 73 to 74F / night 69 to 70F) for a week to push rooting, then widen to day 70 to 75F / night 64 to 68F to steer generative into flower and fruit. [Andrew] (Ch11)
- Always keep day warmer than night; a flat profile pushes vegetative, so avoid it unless the plant needs a strong vegetative push. [Andrew] (Ch11)
- Keep night temperatures at or below 68F (20C) or pepper flowers drop without setting. [Andrew] (Ch11)
- Apply a two to three hour pre-night at or near the night minimum for good set; drop pre-night as low as 61F (16C) to rein in vegetative plants, and ramp 2F every 15 minutes for a really strong generative push. [Andrew] (Ch11)
- Transplant when the king flower is fully developed and never let it set fruit, or the plant may be permanently stunted; always remove the king flower at the split so the plant grows more leaves before carrying load. [Andrew] (Ch11)
- Choose head count by desired fruit size: more heads make smaller fruit, so use two heads for premium large fruit, four where size matters less. Plant two-headed at 3.5 plants/m2 (7 heads/m2), or smaller-fruited at three stems (10.5 stems/m2). [Andrew] (Ch11)
- Prune peppers only every two weeks, or after 6 in (15 cm) of growth or two to three new shoots; do not work the top 8 in (20 cm) until you can tell weak branches from strong. [Andrew] (Ch11)
- Understand load and unload: a plant carries roughly three to six fruits per stem, then aborts flowers until some fruit is harvested; even out flushiness with two-in one-out pruning (two flowers on one stem, one on the other, alternating up). [Andrew] (Ch11)
- On long-season winter crops do not let flowers become fruit until the second or third level; in higher spring light, allow set as early as the first level if plants can handle it. [Andrew] (Ch11)
- Top six or seven weeks before removal to stop vegetative growth and force energy into set fruit; leave a flag leaf to shade fruit and cut sunburn. [Andrew] (Ch11)

### Eggplant (Ch12)

- Treat eggplant as naturally very vigorous and vegetative; lean generative with pre-night and night cooling down to 60 to 64F (15 to 18C) to keep flowering and set strong. Pick a lower-vigor, more generative rootstock and split each plant into at least two leaders to spread the energy. [Andrew] (Ch12)
- Run the tomato temperature program targeting a 66F (19C) average for maximum fruit; keep between 55 and 86F (13 to 30C). Flat 75F (24C) day and night the first week for rooting, then lower 2F per night to 72F day / 66F night, widening to a pre-night differential (day 68 to 72F / night 63 to 66F) as fruit loads. [Andrew] (Ch12)
- Choose taller, less bushy greenhouse varieties so they trellis at high density with good airflow; sow eggplant scions before tomato rootstocks (eggplant germinates slower). [Andrew] (Ch12)
- Transplant at 10 to 12 in (25 to 30 cm) with flowers open or opening (add two weeks if grafted). Set stem density by structure: 3.6 stems/m2 in unheated hoophouses up to 8 in higher-tech houses, reached by developing extra stems (third = 5.4/m2, fourth = 7.2/m2). [Andrew] (Ch12)
- Do not remove side shoots entirely; top each just above its flower cluster so the flowers stay and set. Leave all blossoms in each cluster and all fruit on main stem and suckers; removing suckers can halve yield and push energy into excess vegetative growth. [Andrew] (Ch12)
- In a very low greenhouse grow four stems then top each head a few inches below the top about six weeks after transplant; do not sucker in the week before topping or losing the growing points may trigger shaded base suckers. [Andrew] (Ch12)
- Eggplant lowers and leans harder than tomatoes because stems are brittle; start the plant leaned one position over from vertical in the intended direction. [Andrew] (Ch12)

### Leafy crops and herbs (Ch13)

- Hold lettuce and greens at a 24-hour average of 63 to 65F (17 to 18C); keep below 82F (28C) or the crop turns bitter and bolts. [Andrew] (Ch13)
- Match temperature to light through the seasons: hold cold-tolerant greens slowly at 50 to 54F day / 43 to 47F night in low winter light (almost no growth below 50F), push 59 to 64F day / 50 to 54F night on sunny winter days, and run 66 to 72F day / 59 to 64F night on sunny summer days. [Andrew] (Ch13)
- In summer use shade cloth, whitewash, swamp coolers or fog and the most heat- and bolt-resistant varieties to keep greens under 82F; shade in moderation grows bigger leaves but too little light makes them leggy. [Andrew] (Ch13)
- Avoid hotter-than-ideal or rapidly fluctuating temperatures, which initiate premature bolting before marketable size; pick varieties that hold longest before bolting and are tipburn-resistant under your conditions. [Andrew] (Ch13)
- Plant greens as densely as the variety tolerates, choosing compact airflow-disease-resistant varieties; trial each variety at each spacing since some grow fine at 8 in (20 cm) but rot at 6 in (15 cm). Transplant shallowly so the head base is not buried (buried bases trap moisture and cause bottom rot). [Andrew] (Ch13)
- Harvest healthy plants at proper maturity before they seed; stressed or bolting plants make bitter compounds and off flavors. [Andrew] (Ch13)
- Hold basil at a 24-hour average of 75 to 77F (24 to 25C), best between 70 and 80F, with little DIF (night 65 to 70F); below 50F leaves blacken and die, and heat sends it to seed faster. [Andrew] (Ch13)
- Remove basil flowers as they form to keep it vegetative and lift leaf yield; harvest by cutting above lateral buds so it rebranches, and stagger successions since yield drops once a planting flowers. Set density to light: about 12 plants/m2 in winter, 24 in summer. [Andrew] (Ch13)
- For greenhouse herbs choose fast growth, open habit for airflow, ability to grow under reduced light, compact form, and good color under low light. [Andrew] (Ch13)

## Other (propagation and transplant vigor)

- Move sprouts to heat mats with plenty of light the moment they emerge; seedlings left hot and dark stretch fast, and stems that stretch early may never expand to normal size. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Let unheated-house transplants get big in a heated nursery; more mature transplants begin yielding sooner and cut the time the structure sits idle. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Match pot size to the transplant you want and keep root volume proportional; a big transplant that is root-bound loses its advantage (a 1 quart / 1 l pot suits a big transplant). [Andrew] (Ch6)
