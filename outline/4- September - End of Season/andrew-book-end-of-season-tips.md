## Topping (heading off)

- Topping is cutting off the growing point (head) so upward growth stops and all energy goes to fruit production. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Topped plants ripen a fruit load they otherwise could not support in the dark part of the year, since they no longer sustain vegetative growth. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Topping is done about two months before crop removal. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Seasons are timed so the plants' lowest light needs coincide with the darkest time of year. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Pruning is defined as limiting the amount and type of growth to keep the plant balanced; unlimited heads split a finite energy budget and both growth and ripening slow. [Andrew] (Ch6)
- Fruit left exposed after leaves are stripped ripens and rots faster. [Andrew] (Ch6)


## Fall temperature and light

- Match growing temperature to available light; running a crop hot under low light burns more energy in respiration than weak photosynthesis can replace. [Andrew] (Ch5, Ch13)
- Once a crop stalls from cold or any other stress, recovery takes a long while, so keep plants in continual active growth. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Change greenhouse temperature no faster than 2°F (1°C) per hour. [Andrew] (Ch7)
- Vining/fruiting crops need 20 to 30 mol/m²/day; low light grows leaves, not fruit. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Heating cost is second only to labor cost for many greenhouse growers, and fuel choice has a big impact on the bottom line. Propane and natural gas are the usual choices; electricity and heating oil are rarely cost competitive. [Andrew] (Ch2)
- Greenhouses went out of business in the 1970s energy crisis when spiking fuel costs blew budgets. [Andrew] (Ch2)
- May can give more than 1.4 lb/sq.ft against barely 0.6 lb/sq.ft in October; heating is a yield lever on the sunny months, not only season extension. [Orisha] (Step 5 intro)
- Cloudy days: keep temperature near 60°F day and night. Warmth without light grows thin weak plants. [Orisha] (Step 5 Tip #1)
- Fruit load sets the day target: 8 clusters → ~75°F, 10 clusters late ripening → 70°F, 6 clusters → ~79°F. [Orisha] (Step 5 Tip #2)
- Every degree below target slows growth about 5%; 10°F off is 50% less growth. [Orisha] (Step 2, energy consumption)
- Backup thermostat wired straight to the heater, set 3°F below the main setpoint. Temperature and power alerts on top. [Orisha] (Step 5 Tips #4, #5)
- Pre-nights (a fast drop at the start of the night) steer generative and only work on sunny days with an assimilate surplus. [Andrew] (Ch7)


## Humidity, transpiration, and calcium transport

- Transpiration is the pull that cycles water and nutrients through the plant; when transpiration is not occurring, neither is photosynthesis. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Transpiration is passive in that it depends on the air being able to accept moisture. At 100% RH the air is saturated and transpiration is impossible until RH drops. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Happy medium for most greenhouse crops: VPD between 4 and 8 mbar, RH between 70 and 80%. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Sluggish transpiration slows growth because water and nutrients do not move through the plant fast enough. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Root pressure is the weaker backup force to transpirational pull; when it has nowhere to push it promotes guttation, drops of moisture exuding from the leaves. Guttation is bad and leads to secondary infections. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Chronically high RH and low VPD produce "lazy root": the plant is never stimulated to build roots, and temporary wilting follows. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Blossom end rot is calcium not reaching the end of the developing fruit. It happens with plenty of calcium in the soil, because strongly growing leaves outpull the fruit for the available calcium. Generative steering helps. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Blossom end rot's leathery spots are open doors for botrytis. Remove affected fruit on sight; it cannot recover. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Suspect potassium starvation of fruit after prolonged cool overcast spells, when slow transpiration and reduced roots cannot supply the fruit. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Burnt cucumber heads read as interrupted calcium flow from stress, including low humidity and heavy fruit load. [Andrew] (Ch10)
- Fruit sugar behaves like a fixed potential per fruit: small and concentrated, or large and diluted. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Stop watering two hours before sunset and start two hours after sunrise; standing wet soil at night feeds guttation, splitting, and soil-borne disease. [Andrew] (Ch5)
- Last irrigation no later than 4 hours before sunset. Wet soil overnight grows vines instead of ripening fruit and feeds botrytis and sclerotinia. Check with a finger in the soil before the first morning irrigation. [Orisha] (Step 3 Tip #3)
- Best way to lower humidity: heat with the roll-ups wide open. Best growers pulse air out 6 min every 40 min when humidity is too high. [Orisha] (Step 4 Tip #5)
- Humidity cannot be managed by gut. Use a sensor that logs past humidity, especially overnight. [Orisha] (Step 4 Tip #4)
- Too dry moves with temperature: about 55% RH at 65°F, about 75% RH at 90°F. [Orisha playbook] (`playbook/climate/haf-humidity-and-air-dryness.md`)


## Disease in the last third

- High humidity feeds powdery mildew, gray mold, and leaf mold. Gray mold grows on pruning scars and attacks fruit, cutting shelf life right when the fall harvest lands. [Orisha] (Step 4 intro)
- Aug to Oct yield potential is 30% of the season, about $4,500 per bed. [Orisha] (Step 4 intro)
- Leaf mold hits in July and can cost up to $3,000/bed in lost fall harvest. Rebelski resists both leaf mold and powdery mildew. [Orisha] (Step 1 Tip #1)
- Condensation on plastic drips in the same spot every time, keeping those leaves wet. Anti-condensation liquid can be applied mid-season. [Orisha] (Step 4 Tip #1)
- Morning sun heats air faster than water-filled fruit, so condensation forms on the fruit itself. Gray mold on fruit cuts shelf life from 2 to 3 weeks down to days. Open roll-ups in stages, never fully open at sunrise. [Orisha] (Step 4 Tip #3)
- Break leaves tight to the stem, no stub. Prune mornings on sunny days. Carry a container down the row and take dead material out of the tunnel. [Orisha] (Step 1 Tip #4, Step 4 Tip #8)
- Gray moss visible means it is already sporulating; remove infected tissue on sight. [Orisha] (Step 4 Tip #8)


## Fruit load and harvest standard

- Keep 5 fruit per cluster starting 8 weeks before season end (against 3 through heatwave season). [Orisha] (Step 1 Tip #5)
- Pruning clusters earlier in the season preserves the vigor that carries fall production. [Orisha] (Step 1 Tip #5)
- Set the harvest standard from the supply chain: pick one fruit just barely showing ripe color at the blossom end, taste it, and calibrate. [Andrew] (Ch9)
- Determinate tomatoes, peppers, and eggplant can be run in two shorter cycles, but two cycles a year is usually not economical. [Andrew] (Ch6)
