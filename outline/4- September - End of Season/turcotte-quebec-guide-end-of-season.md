## Fall yield potential

- Sept 1 to Nov 30 potential in Québec: 7 to 12 kg/m² (1.4 to 2.5 lb/sq.ft), depending on greenhouse quality. (Ch4, p69)
- Carrying harvest into December adds only 0.8 to 1.5 kg/m². (Ch4, p69)
- No point chasing more fall yield with more fruit: light caps the total. Too much load in fall gives the same total weight in smaller fruit. (Ch4, p69)
- Set the fruit load from the topping date, the fall light, and the fruit size the market wants. (Ch4, p69)


## Topping (étêtage)

- Top 8 to 12 weeks before the desired last harvest date. Later topping means a longer countback. (Ch4, p73)
- Most Québec growers top between September 15 and October 10. (Ch5, p101)
- Light becomes limiting fast in fall, so the operation cannot be planned late. Top too late and fruit size drops while total yield stays about the same. (Ch4, p74)
- 2 to 3 weeks before topping, steer generative. The last clusters set must be generative and vigorous. Good size is impossible on poorly set clusters. The secret to finishing with first-quality fruit is starting with strong clusters. (Ch4, p73)
- Plant balance at topping is the deciding factor. Too vegetative (many leaves, little fruit) → fruit develops slowly and stays small, and sucker regrowth is heavy. Well reproductive → fruit develops fast and reaches better size, and less sugar goes to new suckers. Less labour, and lower heating cost. (Ch4, p73)
- Fall topping: keep 1 to 2 leaves above the last cluster. Summer topping: 2 to 4. (Ch4, p68, p74)
- Ideally the last cluster kept is already set. Topping above a cluster not yet in flower delays fruit sizing. (Ch4, p68, p74)
- Summer topping only: a month before, build leaf area (keep umbrella suckers) and size up the last 2-3 clusters, up to 6-7 fruit per cluster. (Ch4, p68)


## After topping

- Leaf rules for topped plants: bottom 2 clusters fully cleared; up top keep only 2 leaves per cluster; 8 to 10 mature leaves per plant is enough; as fruit is harvested keep deleafing, down to 6 leaves per plant. (Ch4, p74)
- 5 to 6 leaves per plant is ample after topping. Fruit must be exposed to light. Excess leaves lower fruit size. (Annexe 2, p193)
- Keep managing balance even after topping. Too much vegetation against the fruit load gives micro-cracking and ripening defects. (Ch4, p74)
- Plants turn very dark green after topping. Normal, but avoid an abrupt shift; a good deleafing strategy holds the balance. (Ch4, p74)
- 24 h temperature is set by remaining fruit load. With 5 to 6 clusters left, do not drop below 17 °C (63 °F): ripening drags and harvest quality suffers. With only 3 to 4 clusters left, run cooler to maximize size on the last fruit. (Ch4, p74; Ch5, p101)
- Lower 24 h temperature → longer ripening → bigger fruit. Floor is 17 °C. (Annexe 2, p193)
- Pre-night is recommended only when fruit quality is well controlled. Avoid it if there is growth cracking or micro-cracking. (Annexe 2, p193)
- With 5 clusters left, 22 leaves/m² fully developed is enough. Leaves spread evenly over the full plant height beats bunched. (Ch5, p101)


## Fall crop conduct

- Fall conditions that change the crop: shorter day; less radiation, and worse light penetration because the low sun angle reflects more off the greenhouse; substrate physical properties degraded by the mass of live and dead roots; colder nights; less venting so higher humidity. Net effect: conditions favour fungal disease and push plants vegetative. (Ch4, p70)
- Plant state has changed: very long stems, older and less active roots. Plants respond more slowly to climate changes and are less vigorous. (Ch4, p70)
- Fall objectives: hold enough vigour to form good-quality fruit to the end, keep generative/vegetative balance, keep a steady growth rate, prevent fungal disease. (Ch4, p70)
- Shift the leaf/fruit ratio to the drop in radiation: target 15 to 17 leaves per plant. In fall it is often better to remove leaves from inside the canopy rather than taking them all off the bottom. Light penetration through the whole canopy improves and late in the season that counts. (Ch4, p70)
- Deleafing in the plant head can also be used to push generative. (Ch4, p71)
- Changing the deleafing pattern can hurt biocontrol emergence; compensate with bigger releases. (Ch4, p71)
- Cold nights make the morning heating relaunch important. A good relaunch with a little venting avoids condensation and activates transpiration. (Ch4, p71)
- Bring the heating setpoint close to the venting setpoint. More aggressive management, very effective against heavy air. (Ch4, p71)
- Fall climate should be active: circulation fans, venting, minimum heat. Stagnant air triggers fungal disease. Poor transpiration also causes micro-cracking, marginal leaf burn, and loss of root vigour. (Ch5, p101)
- Deleaf to expose fruit clusters to light, not to strip them bare. Aim for good air movement and light penetration around the fruit. Remove every inactive (too shaded) or yellow leaf. (Ch5, p101)


## Fall fruit load and root health

- Optimal load in early September is around 50 fruit/m² (about 4.6 fruit/sq.ft). Against 55 to 65 fruit/m² for beefsteak in the March to August high-radiation window. (Ch5, p100; Annexe 2, p193)
- Increasing fruit load from early August on is risky. In August, hold it or start bringing it down. From September, it should come down. Too many fruit hurts new cluster formation and lowers fruit size. (Ch5, p100)
- Root loss is the September risk. After a hard summer, plants tolerate management errors less well. A bad irrigation strategy or fruit overload disturbs root growth → root dieback → vigour loss → Pythium takes advantage. (Ch5, p100)
- Fall's natural tendency is vegetative. Very often in August vigour is below optimal and the plant is over-generative; steer vegetative to rebuild it, and cooler nights make that easier. Once vigour is back, resume generative steering to move sugars into the fruit. (Ch5, p100)


## Botrytis in the last third

- A few tissues carrying Botrytis spores in a greenhouse is enough to infect the whole crop. Botrytis can infect and stay latent with no external symptom until conditions favour it. (Ch4, p71)
- Deleaf early in the day, but wait until plants are transpiring. Stop early enough before sunset that wounds dry before night. (Ch4, p72)
- Avoid climate that creates strong root pressure: a plant that transpires little but keeps absorbing water pushes water out through wounds and hydathodes. The droplet at a deleafing or cluster-pruning wound captures airborne Botrytis spores, and when the plant starts transpiring it pulls that spore-loaded droplet back inside, where Botrytis develops sheltered in the tissue. (Ch4, p72)
- Irrigate later in the morning once the plant is transpiring, and finish earlier, so the substrate is not saturated and the plant is not water-congested the next morning. (Ch4, p72)
- Keep fewer leaves per plant and leave no leaves on the ground. (Ch4, p72)
- Keep the soil surface as dry as possible; fix irrigation leaks and water running off under the gutters. (Ch4, p72)
- Heat, ventilate, push humid air out, bring in cooler drier air and heat it. Avoid "cold" ventilation. (Ch4, p72)
- Prevent condensation on the plants at daybreak by not letting air temperature rise too fast. (Ch4, p72)
- Clean every stem canker by cutting out the infected parts, then paint the cleaned area with a registered product. Badly cleaned cankers eat the plant slowly but surely. (Ch4, p72)
- Remove truss stubs (rafles) already yellowed and Botrytis-contaminated. Inspect deleafing nodes and old stubs left on plants. (Ch4, p71, p72)
- Marginal leaf burn is to be avoided. (Ch4, p71)
- If powdery mildew is already in the plants, keep and even reinforce fungicide applications. (Ch4, p71)


## Whitefly gains in fall

- In fall whiteflies gain the advantage over biocontrol for three reasons: radiation drops, there are fewer leaves per plant, and greenhouse temperature is lower. Whiteflies easily overtake Encarsia and Eretmocerus, and the problems start in the following months. Introduce more beneficials in the coming weeks so biocontrol still works in December. (Ch4, p71)


## Fall irrigation and feeding

- From October, cut watering to match the climate and the plant's consumption. (Ch6, p132)
- Emphasis shifts to oxygenating the root zone, because roots are less active at end of production. (Ch6, p132; Ch5, p101)
- Night plus dry-down should drop substrate water content by at least 15%. (Ch6, p132)
- Start irrigating later in the morning, finish earlier in the afternoon. Afternoon watering sparingly. Stop 3 to 4 hours before sunset. (Ch6, p132)
- Longer, less frequent irrigation cycles through the day. (Ch6, p133)
- Raise feed EC to compensate for lower plant activity. Do not cut leaching below 15 to 20% for days on end, or slabs drift apart. (Ch6, p132)
- Use a nutrient solution richer in potassium at the end. (Annexe 2, p193)
- Do not dry the root zone to "stress" the plant for size. Raise EC instead and keep a normal irrigation strategy. Sudden swings from dry to plentiful water cause several quality defects. (Annexe 2, p193)


## End-of-season cleanup (annexe 3)

- Purpose: make the greenhouse as bright as possible, cut phytosanitary risk for the next crop by removing crop residue and weeds and destroying fungal spores, bacteria, and insects, bring all equipment back to performance, and even out heating, venting, and light. Easier to fix defects with no plants in the way. (Ch4, p76; Annexe 3, p250)
- Wash roof, structures, and floor with water or soapy water, sprayer or pressure washer. Do the outside too. The secret is lots of water: 30 to 50 L per 100 m² (0.8 to 1.2 gal per 100 sq.ft). (Ch4, p76; Annexe 3, p250)
- After a soap wash, rinse well. Soap residue cuts the efficacy of some disinfectants, including quaternary ammonium compounds. (Annexe 3, p250)
- Destroy all weeds. (Annexe 3, p251)
- Add a fan wherever stagnant air was observed during the season. (Ch4, p76; Annexe 3, p250)
- Adjust vent opening and closing; calibrate position sensors. (Annexe 3, p251)
- Clean and grease racks, fans, motors. (Ch4, p76)
- Protect heating pipes and greenhouse structures against corrosion. (Ch4, p76)
- Clean and disinfect all equipment: harvest bins, pruners, knives. (Annexe 3, p251)
- Put yellow sticky traps back up for insect detection. Add blue traps if thrips were a problem. (Annexe 3, p251)
- Foot-bath disinfection and hand sanitizer at every greenhouse entrance; a written sanitary protocol for employees and especially visitors. (Annexe 3, p251)
- Heating: check burner or furnace efficiency (a more precise burner setting saves real energy), even out heat distribution, insulate walls and pipes, inspect the whole network for leaks at the steel-to-flex joints. (Ch4, p76; Annexe 3, p252)
- Paint heating pipes white; paint or cover with white plastic any surface that absorbs light it could reflect to the plants. (Annexe 3, p252)
- Water and feed system: check and clean the mixing system, stock tanks, filters, pumps, gauges, check valves; repair every leak; check solenoid valves. (Annexe 3, p252)
- Have the water source analyzed for mineral quality and, if possible, pathogens. For surface water in farm territory, add a herbicide-residue analysis. (Annexe 3, p252)
- Calibrate EC and pH probes, including handhelds. Most pH probes last 12 to 18 months. (Annexe 3, p252)
- Calibrate and clean measuring equipment: air temperature and humidity sensors, and the weather mast (light sensor, anemometer, rain detector). (Annexe 3, p251)
- Reference for disinfection protocol: Liette Lambert, "Le nettoyage et la désinfection des serres en fin de saison," Réseau d'avertissements phytosanitaires, MAPAQ. http://www.agrireseau.qc.ca/rap/?s[0]=0-938-1181-1183-1732&page=1


## Substrate note

- Heavy substrates are harder to handle. Ease of drying down at end of crop, stacking, and disposing of them is a selection criterion. (Ch3)
