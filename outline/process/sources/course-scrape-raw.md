## Step 1 — Start Right (intro)

Yield potential is set at planning stage. Half the right density = half
the harvest. Too dense kills airflow, raises humidity + fungal pressure.
Rule: 1% more light = 1% more yield. Too dense also shades fruit/leaves.

## Step 1 — Tip #1: The Right Variety

Select Leaf Mold (LM) resistant varieties. Focus on Leaf Mold + Powdery
Mildew (PM) for simplicity/impact. Leaf mold hits in July, can cost up to
$3,000/bed in lost fall harvest. Rebelski resists both LM + PM (rare).
Beef: Rebelski (LM/PM), Beorange, Pink ID, Marnouar, Margold. Cherry:
Sakura, Sunpeach, Edox, Golden Sweet. Choose LM-resistant; tackle PM
another way.

## Step 1 — Tip #2: The Right Density

Use leaders/sq.ft, not plants. Two leaders per root system. Beef spacing
18" apart, rows 5 ft apart incl. pathways. 100-ft tunnel = 500 sq.ft bed.
Optimal 0.27 leaders/sq.ft. Create two leaders: at 3rd leaf, pinch above
2nd leaf; the two suckers become the leaders. If missed, keep the sucker
right under a cluster (most vigorous; produces 2-4 wks later). Pattern: 3
leaves, one cluster. Two leaders balance vigor (a too-vigorous plant is
hard to steer to fruit) and cut labor + seed cost.
In-season density correction: remove suckers, prune to 18 leaves/plant,
count hooks (4 per wire on a 5x100, total 8). Too many hooks: pinch excess
leader heads above tied cluster, remove leader after harvest. Too few:
keep extra suckers under a cluster, tie to new hook. South bed target 10
hooks instead of 8.

## Step 1 — Tip #3: Increase Density on the South Bed

South beds unshaded → more light. Increase density 30% (14" apart instead
of 18"). Adjust irrigation (5 drip tapes vs 4) and fertilizer (x1.3).

## Step 1 — Tip #4: Pruning

Prune every week. Ideal balance 18 leaves per leader. Pruning stresses the
plant so it focuses energy on fruit, not leaves. Unpruned = leaves over
fruit, fewer tomatoes, fruit lost in shade taking up to 4 more weeks to
ripen, poor airflow, more disease. Technique: weekly, average leaves over
3 leaders by touching them; if 21 avg, remove lowest 3 leaves per leader
bed-wide. Optimal growth = ~3 leaves/week to prune. Break leaf tight to
stem (no stub → grey mold entry). Prune mornings, sunny days; remove
pruned leaves + harvested-empty clusters from tunnel.

## Step 1 — Tip #5: Control number of fruit per cluster

Keep 3 fruit/cluster 6-8 weeks before heatwave season. Fruit drains energy;
at 95°F too much fruit exhausts the plant → smaller/lower-quality fruit,
longer ripening, slower new clusters, flower abortion, lower season yield.
Itinerary: first two clusters keep 5; next clusters keep 4; 6-8 wks before
heatwave keep 3; 8 wks before season end keep 5. Pinch cluster close to
the last flower kept. Applies to new clusters only; leave ripening ones.

## Step 1 — Tip #6: White Landscape Fabric

White reflects light best (1% light = 1% yield). Use white landscape fabric
in alleys instead of black. Up to 90% reflection when new/clean. Even 5%
reflection pays back season one: 5% of 2 lbs/sq.ft avg = $200/bed vs
$100/bed fabric + labor. Don't use the white side of a silage tarp
(slippery when wet).

## Step 1 — Checklist

Planning: choose LM-resistant varieties; calc plant count; boost south bed
(+30%, 5 drip tapes); prevent errors (south bed 1.3x fertilizer reminder,
post leaves-per-plant tab, write fruit-per-cluster in calendar each week);
order + install white fabric. In-season: post leaves tab, prune leaves +
clusters per tabs, set Dec 1 reminder to review checklist, install white
fabric.

## Step 2 — Get Proper Ventilation (intro)

Plant makes energy via photosynthesis, spends it to grow + maintain roots,
vines, leaves, flowers, fruit. Match consumption to production = healthy
plant. Healthy = lush/vigorous (visible), higher yield (measured), fewer
issues. What healthy looks like:
- Stems one foot below the top as thick as a AA battery
- Highest cluster has 3 tied-up fruit or 3 flowers still attached
- Overall plant strong and lush
Consume > produce → spindly stems, blossom drop, slow tiny fruit, July
yield drop despite sunlight. Step 2 uses ventilation to (1) boost
photosynthesis, (2) align consumption to production. From here on the
course assumes automation (motors on roll-ups + a controller).

## Step 2 — Energy Production (page 2a)

More energy = faster growth without weakening. If the plant elongates into
a thin weak stem, slow growth, or it sacrifices fruit to preserve itself.
First help it produce more energy to keep growth rate high. Three blockers
of energy production: sudden temperature drops, strong direct winds,
over-ventilation. Prevent them to boost growth, especially hot months.

## Step 2 — Tip #1: Prevent Sudden Drop of Temperatures (page 2a-1)

Ventilation must adapt to changing conditions. Sudden temp changes trigger
"protection mode" stopping growth for hours. Keep an 8°F range inside
during the day. A drop of only 10°F can trigger protection mode; several
times/day can delay harvest up to 4 weeks + cause fruit splitting + shelf
life issues. Example day: 6AM sunny open sides; 8:30 cloud → reduce/close
before temp drops 8°F; 9AM sun returns → reopen before temp hits 80°F; 3PM
high winds → reduce/close the wind-exposed side. Automation: motors on
roll-ups + controller (Orisha or Advancing Alternative); install ~2 hrs.

## Step 2 — Tip #2: Protect your Crops from the Wind (page 2a-2)

Open the wind-protected side first. Wind strips the leaf "boundary layer"
(thin humidity film the plant uses to regulate temperature, like sweating);
without it the plant can't transpire properly, so growth stops. Opening the
protected side first uses the chimney effect to suck air out of the tunnel.
If still too hot after fully opening that side, open the other side a little
at a time. Protecting cucumbers from direct wind prevents scarring.
Automation trick: a wind sensor handles this automatically.

## Step 2 — Tip #3: Shut Off your HAF at 86°F (page 2a-3)

As temperature rises, air gets too dry; to save water reserves the plant
stops transpiring, which also stops energy production (happens before
wilting). Lowering ventilation keeps transpired humidity around the canopy.
Do it by shutting off the circulation fans (HAF); their extra airflow is
harmful here. Plants handle heat waves better when they can transpire.
Determining factor is humidity %, and the threshold shifts with temperature.
Close-fans table (temp → humidity threshold): 68°F → 60%, 77°F → 70%,
86°F → 80%, 95°F → 85%.

## Step 2 — Energy Consumption (page 2b)

Tunnel temperature controls growth rate, hence energy consumption; use
ventilation to manage it. Warmer = faster growth. Too hot exhausts energy
storage: plant goes spindly, can't maintain growth, focuses on already-
ripening fruit, new fruits abort (blossom drop before fruit set). Too cold:
plant diverts energy from growth to storage; when storage full (dark, curly
leaves in morning) growth stalls. Every degree below target slows growth 5%;
10°F off = 50% less growth. A manual June tunnel chart showed a 43°F spread
from clouds coming and going. Recommend automating ventilation. Optimal
growth rate varies with conditions; these tips give a safe starting point.
Step 6 covers leaving autopilot to find the best temperature.

## Step 2 — Tip #7: What If Your Ventilation Is Not Powerful Enough (page 2-72)

Heat waves are sometimes unbeatable, but a few high-temp days aren't fatal if
prepared: grew healthy strong heat-resistant plants; pruned clusters to 3
fruit to keep vigor; pruned leaves so plants don't lack water from over-
transpiration and don't waste energy on shaded leaves. Irrigation can help
(Step 3).

## Step 2 — Checklist

Planning season: ensure sufficient ventilation (else order positive-pressure
fans); delegate ventilation to a control system (pays for itself in yield +
quality of life). Growing season: delegate side roll-up to a control system
(2-hr install, big results + workload cut); install positive-pressure fans;
set day temp 75°F, night temp 67°F.

## Step 3 — Irrigate Better (intro)

Water is more than survival. Many farmers irrigate 1-3x/week; high-yielders
irrigate up to 9x/day. Same energy logic as ventilation: under-watering
bottlenecks growth (plant can't cool by transpiration, stops energy
production to hold temperature → growth stops, yield potential drops).
Over-watering causes deficiencies and root disease.
Overwatering signs: pale plant, leggy plant, algae on topsoil, sclerotinia
rot in first 6" of stem, weak plants where water pools, tasteless fruit, new
clusters flowering >7" from the head.
Underwatering signs: pale plant, leggy plant, wilting, soil dry 3" below
ground, new clusters flowering <5" from the head.
Water needs depend on sunlight, soil type, temperature, leaf count. Step 3
gives pointers + a calculator + a 30-sec diagnostic.

## Step 3 — Before We Start (page 3a)

Tips translate water volume into minutes of irrigation, so flow must be
standardized. Recommended setup: 4 drip tapes per bed, holes every 8",
12 psi regulator. (Dubois customer service can design/ship it.) If you keep
your own system, know your water-per-minute and adjust durations. CAUTION:
all numbers assume the Step 1 density; otherwise adapt duration proportionally.

## Step 3 — Tip #1: Adapt Irrigation to Transpiration (page 3-1)

Mature tomato needs 2 ml/joule/cm²/day/m² (too complex to recall). Key: 90%
of water need is transpiration, driven mostly by sun and leaf count. Peak
demand is sunny June days = 45 min/day on the standard setup.
Cloud coverage: ☀️ Sun 100% = 45 min; ⛅️ Partly sunny 66% = 30 min;
☁️ Cloudy 33% = 15 min.
Leaf count (maturity; mature beef = 18 leaves): 18 leaves 100% = 45 min;
9 leaves 50% = 23 min. Tip #6 calculator computes volume by month, cloud,
leaf count.

## Step 3 — Tip #2: Fraction Irrigation Depending on your Soil Type (page 3-2)

Avoid saturating soil (suffocates roots → pale weak plants; flushes minerals,
pollutes water table, raises fertilizer cost). Sandy soil drains fast →
irrigate more often. Clay holds water → spread watering, let it dry a bit
(else roots choke → growth stops, deficiencies, fungal spread).
Irrigations/day by soil — Sand / Loam / Clay: Sun 9/6/3, Partly sunny 5/4/2,
Cloudy 3/2/1. Duration each = total need ÷ number of irrigations. On a sunny
45-min day: Clay 15 min, Loam 7.5 min, Sand 5 min. Tip #6 calculator does it.

## Step 3 — Tip #3: Adapt Irrigation to Sunrise and Sunset Times (page 3-3)

Morning: let plants transpire before first irrigation, else root pressure
rises → fruit splitting and russeting. Wait 3 hours after sunrise for the
first irrigation.
End of day: last irrigation no later than 4 hours before sunset. Wet soil all
night → plant grows vines instead of ripening fruit; wet nights feed botrytis
and sclerotinia.
Check: finger in soil before first morning irrigation must be dry; if still
wet, move last irrigation 30 min earlier and re-check.
Another indicator: first open-flower cluster should sit 5-7" from the top.
<5" → last irrigation 30 min later; >7" → 30 min earlier. Flowering height
shifts within a week; ~6" means you're good.

## Step 3 — Tip #4: Give 30% More Water During Heat Waves (page 3-4)

Without water plants stop energy production to survive. At 95°F give 30% more
water (15 min → 20 min). CAUTION: heat waves end; set a next-morning alarm to
check whether to revert, daily until it's over. Overwatering weakens crop,
splits fruit, promotes disease.

## Step 3 — Tip #5: Use Only 2 Drip Tapes After Transplanting (page 3-5)

First two weeks after transplanting, roots haven't colonized the bed; using
all 4 tapes flushes nutrients. Plant in the middle of the 4 tapes, open only
the 2 middle ones. After two weeks open the 2 outer tapes for the season.
CAUTION: with 2 tapes closed, double each irrigation's duration to give the
same water (4 min → 8 min).

## Step 3 — Tip #6: The Calculator (page 3-6)

Factors: time of year, cloud coverage, density, leaf count, temperature, soil
type, irrigation system flow. Skipping them loses yield; growers raised yields
30-300% by fixing irrigation. Orisha's tool handles the details; they also
offer the calculator with the underlying formula. CAUTION: read "Before We
Start" first for the calculator to make sense.

## Step 3 — Tip #7: Get Irrigation on Auto-Pilot (page 3-7)

Levels of automation (some with remote access). Ideal controller: handles all
Step 3 parameters; no calculator needed, just watch the indicators. Next best:
3 selectable morning scenarios (Rainbird has 3; must hack it past 4
irrigations/day) — configure weekly until 18 leaves, then monthly, using the
Tip #6 calculator. Time-consuming option: single schedule, reconfigure on
every forecast change. Upgrade path: cheap battery-powered controllers, hard
to configure. Payback: proper irrigation boosts production ~30% = +$1,200/bed;
delegating one bed to Orisha earns back 5x the investment.

## Step 3 — Tip #8: The 30-sec Diagnostic (page slug cours-3---8)

Touch test: daytime sun makes plants transpire, so leaves feel cooler than
air. If a grabbed leaf isn't cooler, the plant isn't transpiring → no
photosynthesis → no energy for growth. Use it to tune irrigation. Stabilize
tunnel temperature first (Step 2); sudden changes confuse the diagnosis. Also
put a finger 3" deep into the soil. If everything's right and soil is still
too dry, suspect equipment failure.

## Step 3 — Checklist

Planning season: 4 drip tapes per bed with a 12 psi regulator; order + install
an irrigation controller. Growing season: use the calculator to tune the
program for your setup; order + install a controller (~1 hr); train staff with
the Tip #8 diagnostic.

## Step 4 — The necessity of dehumidifying (intro)

Tunnel humidity runs high (many plants transpiring at once). High humidity is
ideal for disease: powdery mildew, gray mold, leaf mold — typically appear ~
July, by August white powder covers leaves so they can't make energy, then
gray mold grows on pruning scars and attacks fruit. Without energy fruit won't
ripen; when it does, gray mold cuts shelf life → big fall-harvest loss.
Aug-Oct yield potential = 30% of season = $4,500. High humidity also makes
pollen stick to flowers, slowing fruit set and delaying production. Step 4:
tackle condensation, dehumidify, and put every team member on disease watch.

## Step 4 — Tip #1: Anti-condensation treatment (page slug cours-4-1)

Condensation on tunnel plastic does two harms: reduces sunlight (yield loss);
raises disease pressure. Droplets always fall in the same spot, keeping those
leaves wet → ideal for gray mold. Prevent with anti-condensation-treated
plastic (from tunnel manufacturers). If not replacing plastic soon, apply
anti-condensation liquid, even mid-season, no pretreatment needed.

## Step 4 — Tip #2: Use your heater's fan to increase airflow (page 4-2)

Best with a ducted heater. Have an electrician add a switch so the heater's
fan runs without heating, blowing air through the poly tube to prevent
humidity pooling at the plant base. With good pruning + irrigation, nearly
eliminates sclerotinia rot pressure. Not recommended for non-ducted heaters
(they hang close to the crop; blowing air directly dries plants out). CAUTION:
turn this fan off when humidity is too low (see Step 2 Tip #3).

## Step 4 — Tip #3: Smoothen temperature changes in the morning (page 4-3)

Morning sun heats tunnel air fast; water-filled fruit warms slower, so
condensation forms on fruit. Slow-evaporating droplets let gray mold grow →
small black spots, fruit rots in storage (shelf life days vs 2-3 weeks).
Solution: a reactive system that opens roll-ups in stages following
temperature, never fully open at sunrise (cold shock). Example: one roll-up
opened in 16 stages, a second contributed gradually until 10:15 am, keeping
temperature in the target range and avoiding fruit condensation.

## Step 4 — Tip #4: Get a sensor (page 4-4)

Can't manage humidity by gut; need current and past humidity, especially at
night. Use a sensor, ideally with a past-humidity graph: better diagnostics,
clear dehumidification path, understanding temp↔humidity relationship. Cheap
sensors exist; ideal is a controller that acts on humidity. (On the graph the
lower the line, the higher the humidity.) Example showed morning humidity
problems — 4 hours in the danger zone let powdery mildew/gray mold spores
germinate. Step 5 covers using this info.

## Step 4 — Tip #5: Pulse out air every 40 min (page 4-5)

Best way to lower humidity: heat while roll-ups are wide open. Warm air
escapes the sides, drawing in drier outside air; the heater prevents cold
shock for smooth exchange. Best growers pulse air out 6 min, repeated every
40 min when humidity is too high. Three adjustments: pulse duration; roll-up
opening; time between pulses. Goal: pull humidity out of the danger zone at
each pulse to stop most disease spores germinating and ruining fall harvest.
(Lower graph line = higher humidity.)

## Step 4 — Tip #6: Train your workers to spot diseases (page 4-6)

Alert staff while pruning catch disease early. Print a color picture of main
diseases + pests, hang at eye level in the tunnel, write what to do on it.
Teach the dangers of condensation; ask workers to report droplets from
plastic, condensation on fruit, or on leaves. Everyone contributes to
prevention.

## Step 4 — Tip #7: Weekly inspection (page 4-7)

Assign one person to a slow weekly inspection: walk every path both ways,
bend to check under leaves, spot-check clusters. Look for: 1) white spots
(powdery mildew); 2) yellow leaves (deficiencies); 3) wilting (sclerotinia);
4) gray moss on leaves/stem/fruit (gray mold); 5) condensation on
plastic/fruit/leaves; 6) whether any applied treatment is working; 7) puddles
of stagnant water. Stay ahead of problems and act before losses.

## Step 4 — Tip #8: Taking the dead plant material out as you prune (page 4-8)

Dead material on the ground rots → ideal for gray mold (Botrytis). Gray moss =
already sporulating; remove infected tissue (fruit, leaves, stems) as soon as
found. Push a container in front of you while pruning, drop leaves in, empty
outside the tunnel when full. Pushing it forward prevents forgotten trips;
ideal container holds one row of pruned leaves. Empty at each row's end.

## Step 4 — Checklist

Planning season: order a humidity sensor or dehumidification controller; apply
anti-condensation liquid on plastic; create disease-ID + action documents for
the team. Growing season: with Orisha, order Disease Prevention (no install);
without, order a sensor + humidity controller and pulse air out manually until
it arrives; create the disease-ID documents.

## Step 5 — Heat to unlock growth (intro)

Heating isn't just season extension; it's a lever to use the sunniest months.
Start heat control early to capture high-light months. May can give >1.4
lb/sq.ft vs barely 0.6 lb/sq.ft in October. Also: better dehumidification
control (healthier crops longer) and balance between growth and fruit for
steady, higher total yields. Heating is a yield booster, not a cost — path to
up to 9 lbs/ft². Not just survival: needed all season for optimal temperature;
too-low temps slow growth. Early months should make one new cluster/week;
under-heating ("plant isn't dying") slows cluster generation and cuts whole-
season yield potential.

## Step 5 — Tip #1: Adjust the temperature depending on cloud coverage (page 5-1)

Leggy, thin-stemmed summer plants come from poor energy balance. Pruning
clusters (Step 1) helps but isn't enough; high summer temps + heavy fruit are
very energy-demanding and need lots of sun. On cloudy days light is short:
keep temperature near 60°F day and night. Warmth forces growth; without light
that growth is thin and weak, so cloudy days need cooler temps to keep the
balance that sets lots of fruit while growing new clusters. Break the balance
and you get a few good weeks, then a slowdown until fruit load drops — big lost
yield potential. Check: stem size 12" below the leader head (see Step 2).

## Step 5 — Tip #2: More clusters = lower temperature (page 5-2)

Fruits are energy sinks; more fruit = less heat tolerance. (Analogy: running
at 80°F with an empty backpack is easy; add bricks and it exhausts — full
clusters are bricks, which is why Step 1 prunes them before heat waves.) Rule
of thumb: keep day temp ~75°F for an 8-cluster plant. 10 clusters (late
ripening) → aim 70°F; 6 clusters → ~79°F to force energy into fruit. Check:
stem size 12" below the leader head (see Step 2).

## Step 5 — Tip #3: Use ducted heater (page 5-3)

Best heat distribution = ducts on the heater. Steps: attach a square-to-round
metal adapter to the heater front; install a metal main duct the full tunnel
length; add tee connectors (one per pathway) for poly-tube outlets; run poly
tubes down the walkways, same length as the tunnel; punch holes every 18" on
both sides, horizontal so warm air blows sideways along the crop base. Makes
the tunnel more uniform and stops the front row drying out. Not all heaters
can be ducted — get one with a blower, not a fan.

## Step 5 — Tip #4: Use backup thermostat (page 5-4)

Something fails eventually (power glitch, disconnected controller, hardware).
Wire a backup thermostat directly to the heater — plug it in the same place as
the controller. If the primary fails, the backup prevents a freeze-out. Set
the backup 3°F below the main setpoint so it only kicks in on failure without
interfering with the normal target.

## Step 5 — Tip #5: Set up alerts (page 5-5)

Heaters are growers' #1 stress factor; a failure at the wrong time can lose a
crop overnight. Get alerts for temperature drops, power loss, system
disconnections so you have time to act. Options: AcuRite temperature alerts
(text/email); Orisha built-in alerts (temp highs/lows, power outages).
CAUTION: one failure is enough to regret not having alerts; they don't fix
problems but give you the chance to.

## Step 5 — Checklist

Planning season: time planting for spring light (generally March-April);
select + install a reliable heater sized for the tunnel; add ducts if it uses
a blower; install a backup thermostat 3°F below the main target; set up
temperature alerts (AcuRite, Orisha); plan temperature targets by fruit load
(Tip #2/#3); print a fruit-load vs temperature chart for the tunnel.
Growing season: include heating in dehumidification pulses; adjust temperature
target by current fruit load and daily sunlight; watch stem size 12" below the
head (thicker than an AA battery = energy not used efficiently → adjust temp);
if heater has no ducts, shop a blower model before next season; install a
backup thermostat 3°F below the main target.
